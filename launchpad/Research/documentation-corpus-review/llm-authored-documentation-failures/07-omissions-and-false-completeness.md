## Executive answer

**Agents omit prerequisites, constraints, failure modes, edge cases and negative behavior for one structural reason and one incentive reason, and the two compound.**

The structural reason is that **absence is not a retrievable or attendable object.** AbsenceBench measures this directly: given both an original document and an edited copy with pieces removed — a strictly easier setup than authoring, where no original exists — the best model tested, Claude-3.7-Sonnet, reaches only 69.6% F1 at an average context length of about 5K tokens, while the same class of model is near-ceiling on Needle-in-a-Haystack recall. The authors attribute this to Transformer attention having no key corresponding to a gap, and report that inserting explicit placeholders where content was removed substantially improves performance ([Fu et al., NeurIPS 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11440)). Interpretation: an agent drafting documentation cannot notice its own omissions by rereading its draft, because there is nothing in the draft to attend to. The same asymmetry survives into review — an LLM judge scoring clinical notes discriminated inserted or altered content at 0.79–0.94 but omitted content at only 0.50–0.63, "near chance" ([Fox et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.31016), preprint).

The incentive reason is that **"appears comprehensive" is the quantity that training and evaluation actually optimize.** Kalai et al. argue that hallucination persists because "language models are optimized to be good test-takers, and guessing when uncertain improves test performance", calling the resulting pressure an "epidemic" of penalizing uncertain responses ([arXiv:2509.04664](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664)). Reward models correlate strongly with response length, to the point that "a purely length-based reward reproduces most downstream RLHF improvements" ([Singhal et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03716)). And LLM judges "have powerful implicit biases, prioritizing style over factuality and safety": reformatting a top response to be concise cost 63% of its score while making it factually incorrect cost 13% ([Feuer et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.15268v1)).

**The two meet at a specific place: the field operationalizes documentation completeness structurally.** DocAgent's completeness metric "measures the extent to which the generated documentation adheres to standard structural conventions and includes essential components expected for a given code element" — the presence of Summary, Description, Args, Returns, Raises, Examples ([Yang et al., ACL 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08725v1)). Dvivedi et al. score completeness by counting satisfied checklist items and find GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 "significantly outperform even the original documentation in terms of completeness and thoroughness" ([AIware 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.10349)). This is my central interpretation: **an agent that fills every section of a documentation template scores as complete under the measures the field uses, and a filled template is exactly what a reader reads as comprehensive.** False completeness is not a failure to satisfy the completeness metric; it is what satisfying the completeness metric looks like when the metric is structural.

Three secondary mechanisms are separately evidenced. Scope-limiting detail is dropped systematically when models compress: across 4,900 summaries from 10 models, LLM summaries were "almost 5 times as likely to contain generalized conclusions" than human ones (OR = 4.85, 95% CI [3.06, 7.70], p < 0.001), and — the finding that matters most for mitigation design — prompting explicitly for faithfulness "produced a twofold increase in the likelihood of generalized conclusions" ([Peters & Chin-Yee, *R. Soc. Open Sci.* 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12042776/)). Content that *is* present in the context still gets dropped during extraction — Barnett et al.'s FP7, "Incomplete", covers answers that "miss some of the information even though that information was in the context and available for extraction" ([CAIN 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05856)). And the evidence corpus is itself positively biased: in this repository, 170 environment variables are read by the tracked Rust source and 19 appear in `.env.example`, 49 are named in no tracked Markdown file at all, and only 22 of 266 declared error-message literals appear anywhere in 17 MB of tracked Markdown (commands and revision below).

**The material qualification on all of this** is that no source measures omission in agent-authored repository documentation. The direction is supported from four independent settings and is, in my judgement, robust; the magnitudes are not transferable. The one counterevidence finding that genuinely bounds the answer is Dvivedi et al.'s: on the checklist definition of completeness, LLM-generated documentation already beats the human documentation it replaces. The problem this report names is therefore not "agents are less complete than people" — it is that the completeness we can measure and the completeness a reader needs are different quantities, and agents are unusually good at the first.

## Failure taxonomy

Classes are separated by *why the material is missing*, because that determines which check could catch it. Class names are mine; the evidence column is sourced.

## Causes and mechanisms

### Sourced: absence has no representation to attend to

This is the load-bearing mechanism, and it is measured rather than argued. AbsenceBench gives models both an original document and an edited copy and asks which pieces were removed — across numerical sequences, poetry, and GitHub pull requests. Claude-3.7-Sonnet reaches 69.6% F1 "with a modest average context length of 5K tokens", against near-ceiling performance by the same class of model on Needle-in-a-Haystack retrieval of *present* information. The authors' explanation is architectural: attention operates over keys, and a gap produces no key. Their diagnostic confirms it — "explicitly inserting placeholders where content is missing substantially improves performance" ([Fu et al., NeurIPS 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11440)).

Interpretation, and the reason this matters more for authoring than the benchmark implies: AbsenceBench is the *easy* case. The model is handed the original. A documentation agent has no original — the "document that should have been written" does not exist anywhere — so the omission it must notice is not a diff against an available reference but a diff against an unwritten ideal. If detection is at 69.6% F1 with the reference in context, the authoring case has no reason to be better and every reason to be worse.

### Sourced: the incentive gradient runs toward apparent comprehensiveness

Three independent findings point the same way.

Kalai et al. locate hallucination in evaluation design rather than in model internals: models "guess when uncertain, producing plausible yet incorrect statements instead of admitting uncertainty", and this persists because "most evaluations are graded" such that "guessing when uncertain improves test performance"; they name the result an "epidemic" of penalizing uncertain responses and argue the remedy is socio-technical — rescoring the dominant benchmarks rather than adding hallucination benchmarks ([arXiv:2509.04664, 4 September 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664)).

Singhal et al. show the preference-learning stage carries a length gradient: "length correlates strongly with reward", "improvements in reward score are driven in large part by shifting the distribution over output lengths", and "a purely length-based reward reproduces most downstream RLHF improvements over supervised fine-tuned models", with reward models identified as the dominant source ([arXiv:2310.03716](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03716)).

Feuer et al. show the judging stage does the same. Introducing controlled violations into top-scoring responses, they measure score losses of 96% for a sarcastic tone, 63% for concise reformatting, 13% for factual incorrectness, and 8% for bland style, concluding that "LLM-judges have powerful implicit biases, prioritizing style over factuality and safety" and that judges "implicitly favor completeness and style" ([arXiv:2409.15268v1, September 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.15268v1)).

Interpretation: each of these is a gradient toward output that is longer, more elaborate, more confidently stated and structurally richer. None of them is a gradient toward output that names its own gaps. An agent trained and evaluated on this stack will, when uncertain whether a prerequisite exists, produce a fluent section rather than a flag — and will be scored better for it.

### Sourced: completeness, as the field measures it, is structural

DocAgent's completeness metric checks for the presence of "Summary, Description, Args, Returns, Raises, Examples, Attributes for classes" based on the element's signature, body and visibility, and the authors state that "Completeness was omitted from the ablation study because it depends on the code's structure, not the Navigator's processing order" ([Yang et al., ACL 2025 demo](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08725v1)). Dvivedi et al. rate completeness 0–5 by counting satisfied checklist items — for a file, whether "Brief description of the file/script's purpose is included" and whether a "List of dependencies is provided" — and report that GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 "significantly outperform even the original documentation in terms of completeness and thoroughness" (p = 0.0030 for the model effect) across 14 Python snippets from repositories including Bitcoin, TensorFlow and SQLAlchemy ([AIware 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.10349)). Off-the-shelf tooling measures the same shape: `interrogate` "will tell you which methods, functions, classes, and modules have docstrings, and which do not" ([interrogate documentation, retrieved 2026-09-08](https://interrogate.readthedocs.io/)).

Interpretation, and my central claim: this is not a defect in any of these instruments — presence is what can be checked deterministically. It is a defect in what the ecosystem then means by "complete". A `Raises:` section that names the two exceptions the agent saw satisfies the metric exactly as well as one naming all eight. The metric, the reader's visual scan, and the LLM judge's structural preference all read the same signal, and none of them is reading the thing that would fail.

### Sourced: compression drops the qualifier before it drops the claim

Peters & Chin-Yee tested 10 models over 4,900 summaries (4,300 abstract summaries, 600 article summaries) and classified three shifts — generic generalizations, present-tense generalizations, and action-guiding generalizations — all of which arise from dropping scope-limiting detail. Overgeneralization rates ran 26–67% (DeepSeek), 45–60% (ChatGPT-4o) and 69–73% (LLaMA 3.3 70B), against −1 to 20% for Claude models; against human comparators, "LLM summaries were almost 5 times as likely to contain generalized conclusions compared to *NEJM JW* summaries (OR = 4.85, 95% CI [3.06, 7.70], *p* < 0.001)"; and "newer models tended to perform worse in generalization accuracy than earlier ones" ([*R. Soc. Open Sci.* 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12042776/)).

Interpretation: the sentence "in our tests on Postgres 16 the migration completed in under a second" and the sentence "the migration is fast" differ by exactly the material this report is about. Compression is not neutral over that difference — it removes the bounding clause and keeps the assertion, because the assertion is the informative part by every summarization objective. The documentation consequence is that constraints and edge cases are the *first* casualties of any length pressure, not a random sample of them.

### Sourced and reproducible: the evidence corpus is positively biased

Documentation records what exists. Code records what happens. Neither records what does not exist, is unsupported, or was deliberately not built. Measured on this repository at commit `a7948eea92e13d6a30f00efe9fefc71308a2cb25` on 2026-09-08 (this worktree is sparse — 613 of 6,288 tracked files are on disk — so all probes query git objects):

Applying the same test to error-message literals — deduplicated, format placeholders stripped, and restricted to literals of at least 12 characters so that short strings cannot match incidentally — 22 of 266 appear anywhere in the tracked Markdown.

Interpretation, bounded: this is a mostly human-authored upstream corpus, so it is evidence about the *input* an agent would work from, not about agent output. What it demonstrates is the shape of the problem. An agent asked to write a configuration reference and grounded in `.env.example` and the READMEs would produce a structured, defaulted, plausible document covering 19 variables out of 170 — and 49 of the missing ones are not named anywhere in the corpus, so no amount of care applied to the retrieved documentation would surface them. The same holds for failure modes: an agent grounded in the prose has 22 error conditions available to it; an agent grounded in the code has 266 and no principled basis for choosing which to document. Note also that `.env.example` opens with "All defaults here work with `docker compose up` out of the box" — a completeness-suggesting sentence about a file that covers 11% of the variables the code reads.

### Interpretation: why documentation makes omission uniquely durable

Three properties, offered as interpretation and not directly measured by any source found:

1. **Omission has no failure signal at authoring time.** A wrong statement can be contradicted by the repository; a missing statement contradicts nothing. The artifact is internally consistent, passes every link and lint check, and fails only later, in a reader's environment, where the failure is attributed to the system rather than to the document.
2. **The reader cannot distinguish "not applicable" from "not investigated".** A document with no *Limitations* section and a document whose limitations were checked and found to be none are byte-similar. Silence is not a marked state.
3. **Structural conventions terminate generation.** Documentation genres have canonical section sets. Filling them is a well-defined stopping condition, and it is uncorrelated with whether the determining information has been transferred. This is the authoring-side counterpart of O7.

| Method | What it measures | What it cannot establish |

| **Enumerate-then-verify** — list the facts the source establishes, then check each against the document ([Fox et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.31016)) | Converts an absence question into a series of presence checks, which is the operation models can do. Measured recovery: a monolithic judge reached 8.3% omission detection at a 6.5% false-alarm rate; a pipeline reached 24.6% at 2.7% and named the specific missing fact; an evolved prompt reached 36.9% at 6.2% | Only finds omissions of facts the enumerator produced, so it inherits the enumerator's blind spots exactly. The local probes above are an instance: it is cheap to compute that 170 variables are read and 19 documented | Cannot judge whether an omission was *correct*: most of 170 variables should not be in a user-facing reference. Produces a list, not a priority. Silent on O1, O2, O4, O5 — none of which has a mechanical enumerator |

Directly motivated by the finding that pre-installation instruction is the largest and most-revised README category ([Gao et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.03250v1)) | Establishes only that *the documented path* works in *the tested environment*. | **Structural / coverage checks** — required sections, docstring coverage ([interrogate](https://interrogate.readthedocs.io/)), DocAgent-style completeness ([Yang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08725v1)) | Presence of expected components. Cheap, deterministic, useful as a floor | Measures exactly the quantity that O7 exploits. ### The asymmetry that governs all of these

Interpretation, following from the mechanism: **every method above is a test that a *named* item is present. None is a test that nothing important is unnamed.** Enumerate-then-verify works precisely because it performs that conversion, and its ceiling is set by the enumeration. The practical consequence is that omission checking is only as good as the inventory it is run against, and the inventory must come from somewhere outside the document — code, schema, execution, or a person who knows the system. A completeness check run against the document's own table of contents is circular and will always pass.

A second consequence: **the check that is cheapest to run is the one that most reliably produces the wrong signal.** Structural coverage is trivially automatable and it is the exact metric a template-filling agent maximizes. Reporting a coverage percentage next to an agent-authored document does not qualify the document; it corroborates the illusion.

## Mitigations

### Supported, with measured effect

- **Restructure the review task from absence-checking to presence-checking.** Enumerate the facts the source establishes, then verify each against the document as a closed check. Measured: 8.3% → 24.6% (pipeline, at a *lower* 2.7% false-alarm rate) and → 36.9% (evolved prompt, 6.2% false alarms), with human validation confirming the pipeline found real omissions the best monolithic judge missed ([Fox et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.31016)). Boundary: one clinical study, preprint, and detection remains under 40% — this improves the odds materially without making omission reliably detectable.
- **Insert explicit placeholders for what is not covered.** AbsenceBench reports that inserting placeholders where content was removed "substantially improves performance", which is their evidence for the attention explanation ([Fu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11440)). Interpretation: the documentation analogue is a mandatory, non-empty *Not covered* or *Known failure modes* slot — an omission that is written down becomes a token the model and the reader can attend to. Boundary: the source measures detection of removed text, not authoring; the transfer is mine.
- **Permit and reward abstention explicitly.** First-party guidance: "Allow Claude to say 'I don't know': Explicitly give Claude permission to admit uncertainty. This simple technique can drastically reduce false information", alongside quote-first grounding and per-claim citation with retraction of unsupported claims ([Anthropic, *Reduce hallucinations*, retrieved 2026-09-08](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-hallucinations)). The same document carries its own bound: "while these techniques significantly reduce hallucinations, they don't eliminate them entirely." Kalai et al.'s systemic version is to rescore the benchmarks that penalize abstention ([arXiv:2509.04664](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664)), which is not available to a documentation reviewer.
- **Generate the required-topic inventory from the artifact, not from the genre.** Supported by the local probes as feasible, and by DocAgent's design principle that repository structure — not a prose template — should drive what gets documented ([Yang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08725v1)).

### Where mitigations backfire or reverse

- **Prompting for accuracy or faithfulness can make scope omission worse.** This is the most important negative result found. Peters & Chin-Yee report that "asking for responses faithful to the original text produced a twofold increase in the likelihood of generalized conclusions", raising algorithmic overgeneralizations by up to 15% ([*R. Soc. Open Sci.* 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12042776/)). Interpretation: an instruction to be accurate reads, at generation time, as an instruction to be clean and assertive. A review programme that relies on "we told it to be thorough" has no evidence behind it and one direct measurement against it.
- **Required-section templates convert into the failure they were meant to prevent.** A mandatory *Limitations* heading raises the structural completeness score whether or not it is populated with anything determining — which is O7 restated. Interpretation: template requirements should be paired with a check that the section's content resolves against the artifact, or they trade one illusion for a tidier one.
- **More completeness is not uniformly better, and the trade is real.** Judges penalize conciseness heavily ([Feuer et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.15268v1)) and reward models reward length ([Singhal et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03716)), which means "document everything" pushes in the direction the model is already biased. Documenting all 170 environment variables found locally would produce a reference nobody can use. The judgement about which omissions matter is a reader-fitness judgement (topic 11) and is not automatable from the artifact.
- **Enumeration inherits its enumerator's gaps.** Converting absence to presence works, but a fact the enumerator never extracted is invisible to every downstream check, and the residual is silent rather than flagged.

### Counterevidence, carried rather than resolved

- **Agents may already be more complete than the documentation they replace.** Dvivedi et al.'s checklist evaluation finds GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 "significantly outperform even the original documentation in terms of completeness and thoroughness", with Bard and Llama 2 roughly matching it ([AIware 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.10349)). Taken at face value this contradicts a naive reading of this report. I do not think it does: it measures checklist satisfaction, which is precisely the quantity O7 says an agent maximizes. But it does bound the claim — the defensible statement is that agent documentation is *structurally* more complete and that its substantive completeness is unmeasured, not that it is worse.
- **Omission is not always the dominant risk.** In an evaluation of 100 emergency-department encounter summaries, 47% of GPT-4 summaries omitted clinically relevant information and 42% exhibited hallucinations — comparable rates — while mean potential harm was low, 0.57 (SD 1.11) out of 7 ([Williams et al., *PLOS Digital Health* 2025](https://journals.plos.org/digitalhealth/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pdig.0000899)). Interpretation: omission is frequent, and frequency is not severity; a review programme that treats every omission as equivalent will spend its budget badly.
- **Human reviewers may not share the machine asymmetry**, per the planted-error result cited above. If that holds, human review is not merely a fallback for omission — it is the control with a qualitatively different profile, and substituting an LLM judge for it removes the only check that was working on this class.

## Limits and open questions

- **No measurement exists for the actual question.** Nobody has published an omission rate for agent-authored repository documentation, by class or in aggregate. Everything quantitative here is transferred from clinical notes, scientific abstracts, synthetic absence tasks, or docstring checklists. The direction is supported by four independent settings; no magnitude in this report should be quoted as applying to documentation agents.
- **The transfer direction of the AbsenceBench result needs stating carefully.** It measures detection *with the original available*, which is easier than authoring; I have argued the authoring case is worse, but that is inference, not measurement. It is conceivable that authoring-time attention to a code artifact behaves differently from diff-detection over prose.
- **The detection evidence rests substantially on one 2026 preprint** in a clinical domain, not peer-reviewed at the time of writing and not replicated. It is the source of the omission/commission discrimination figures, the recovery figures, and the human-comparison claim. If it does not replicate, the *Detection* section's quantitative content largely goes with it, though the qualitative asymmetry would still be supported by AbsenceBench.
- **The local probes measure a surface, not a behavior.** 170 vs 19 environment variables and 22 of 266 error literals characterize what an agent would inherit from this corpus. They are not evidence that any agent omitted anything, and the matching heuristics are crude — a variable can be documented under a different name, and an error condition can be described without quoting its message. Treat these as upper bounds on the gap and as a demonstration that mechanical inventories are cheap to build.
- **The counterevidence is not fully resolved.** I have argued that Dvivedi et al.'s result is compatible with this report's thesis rather than contrary to it, but that argument rests on my reading of what their metric measures. A study that scored substantive rather than structural completeness against a code-derived ground truth would settle it, and I found none.
- **Attribution of cause is partly inferential.** The incentive evidence (Kalai, Singhal, Feuer) establishes that the training and evaluation stack rewards long, confident, structurally rich output. That this is *why* a particular documentation omission occurred is interpretation. An alternative explanation — that omission is dominated by evidence selection upstream (topic 01) rather than by generation-time incentives — is not excluded by anything here, and both are probably operating.
- **Unresolved.** Does an artifact-derived required-topic inventory improve substantive completeness enough to justify the verbosity it induces? Is there a detectable textual signature that distinguishes "this section was checked and is empty" from "this section was never investigated"? Do the O1/O2 classes, which have no mechanical enumerator, respond to any automated check at all, or are they irreducibly a human control? Does the machine/human omission-detection asymmetry hold outside clinical notes?
- **Mutability.** Vendor guidance and model behavior change. First-party citations are dated to retrieval on 2026-09-08; the model-specific figures (Claude-3.7-Sonnet, GPT-4, LLaMA 3.3 70B) describe those versions and should not be read forward, particularly given the finding that newer models scored *worse* on generalization accuracy than earlier ones.

## Practical review checks

Research output, offered as candidates. None has been trialled here and none is adopted policy.

- **Never report a structural completeness score as a completeness result.** Coverage of required sections is the metric the failure mode maximizes; publishing it beside an agent-authored document corroborates the illusion rather than testing it.
- **Build the required-topic inventory from the artifact before reading the document.** Declared errors, environment variables read, config keys, exit codes, public API surface — mechanically extracted at a pinned revision, then reviewed as a checklist. The local commands above are a working template.
- **Run review as enumerate-then-verify, not as read-and-judge.** List what the source establishes, then check each item against the document. This is the one intervention with a measured effect on omission detection, and it also makes the residual explicit: what the enumeration missed is what remains unchecked.
- **Require a non-empty *Not covered* section, and check that it resolves.** An omission that is written down is attendable; a blank one is not. Pair it with a check that its entries correspond to real artifacts, or it becomes another filled box.
- **Treat prerequisites as executable, not editorial.** The only reliable test for an unstated precondition is running the procedure in a clean environment. Absence of a prerequisite is invisible to every reading-based check, including a careful one.
- **Do not rely on instructing the agent to be thorough or faithful.** There is a direct measurement that a faithfulness instruction roughly doubled scope-dropping in a comparable compression task. Instructions of this shape should be treated as unevidenced until tested on the specific artifact class.
- **Route O1, O2 and O5 — prerequisites, usage constraints, negative behavior — to a human who knows the system.** None has a mechanical enumerator, and the one available signal on human reviewers suggests they do not share the machine asymmetry on omission.
- **Ask, per section, "what would have to be true for this section to be finished, and was that checked?"** A completeness judgement that never names what it would have expected to find is not a completeness judgement.
- **Prefer "no omission detected against inventory X" to "complete".** The wording should carry the inventory, because the inventory is the whole strength of the claim.
- **Distinguish frequency from severity when triaging omissions.** Comparable omission and hallucination rates coexisted with low mean harm in the one study that scored both; treating every omission as blocking will exhaust the review budget on the wrong ones.
