## Executive answer

**Reader and task fitness fails for a reason that is structural rather than incidental: the quantity that defines fitness is a difference, and an agent has direct access to only one of its two terms.** Google states the target as a subtraction — "good documentation = knowledge and skills your audience needs to do a task − your audience's current knowledge and skills" ([Google, *Technical Writing One*](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/audience)) — and ISO/IEC/IEEE 26514:2022 makes establishing the subtrahend a process requirement, describing "how to establish what information users need" and naming the specialists "who identify the tasks that the intended users can perform with the software" ([IEEE SA, 26514-2022](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/26514/7467/)). An agent authoring from repository evidence can read the minuend — what the system requires — off the source. The subtrahend is not in the repository. Nothing the agent retrieves tells it what its reader already knows or what decision the reader is currently stuck on. **Interpretation:** with one operand unobservable, the agent does not compute a difference at all; it emits the minuend, and the minuend is a complete, self-contained, audience-neutral account of the system. That artifact is generic, long, pitched at no particular level, and organized around the system rather than the reader's task — which is the failure this topic names, arrived at without any error of fact.

**Three measured biases push the same direction rather than correcting it.** Length: Singhal et al. find "improvements in reward to largely be driven by increasing response length" and that "even a purely length-based reward reproduces most downstream RLHF improvements over supervised fine-tuned models" ([arXiv:2310.03716](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03716)); Dubois et al. show the same confound in evaluation, where controlling for length raises Spearman correlation with Chatbot Arena from 0.94 to 0.98 ([arXiv:2404.04475](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.04475)). Typicality: the Verbalized Sampling authors identify "typicality bias in preference data, whereby annotators systematically favor familiar text," and explicitly locate it at the data level rather than in the algorithm ([arXiv:2510.01171](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01171)); Kirk et al. find RLHF "substantially reduces output diversity" both per-input and across-input relative to SFT ([ICLR 2024](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/5a68d05006d5b05dd9463dd9c0219db0-Paper-Conference.pdf)). Addressee: Sharma et al. find that matching a user's views "is one of the most predictive features of human preference judgments" ([arXiv:2310.13548](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)). **Interpretation:** length bias makes long the default, typicality bias makes modal the default, and sycophancy points the residual optimization pressure at the person who issued the authoring request — who is, in almost every documentation task, not the reader. The reader is absent from the loop at training time and absent again at authoring time.

**The repository shows the same failure in its own audience metadata, and shows it as a declaration problem rather than a prose problem.** At the recorded revision, `launchpad/docs/corpus/schema/node.schema.json` requires an `audiences` array drawn from a closed four-value enum (`agent`, `developer`, `operator`, `reviewer`). Of 381 tracked Markdown files under `launchpad/docs`, 370 declare that field; **278 of the 370 (75.1%) name three or more of the four permitted audiences, and 79 name all four.** Only 20 name exactly one. The schema's own invalid fixtures — `missing-audiences.md`, `unknown-audience-value.md`, `duplicate-audiences.md` — show what is checked: presence, membership, uniqueness. Nothing checks that the prose is pitched to the declared reader, and nothing could. The corpus's entry-point node anticipated the drift in prose: it argues for excluding `agent` from its own `audiences` because listing it "would make an audience-filtered view return two nodes to a cold-start agent, one of which only redirects to the other," describes itself as the corpus's "first deliberate audience *exclusion*", and concedes that "[a] reader who thinks `audiences` should name everyone who might read a node rather than everyone it addresses would add `agent` back" (`launchpad/docs/corpus/README.md`). The corpus-wide distribution shows the permissive reading won by roughly three to one.

**The reader-harm evidence is real but weaker than the mechanism evidence, and it constrains how strongly this report can be stated.** Uddin and Robillard's two IBM surveys (323 professionals; 179 documentation examples over 131 documentation units) catalogue exactly the fitness failures — bloat, fragmentation, excess structural information, tangled information — but rank them *below* the content problems: all six problems ever rated "Blocker" were content problems, and "no one selected" tangled information or excessive structural information as a top problem ([IEEE Software 32(4), 2015](https://www.cs.mcgill.ca/~martin/papers/ieeesw2015.pdf)). Aghajani et al.'s survey of 146 practitioners puts documentation clarity at 88% importance and "content is not useful in practice" at 59%, but superfluous content at 55%, duplicate content at 46%, and format/presentation at only 33% ([ICSE 2020](https://emadpres.github.io/pdfs/icse2020.pdf)). **Interpretation:** verbosity and mispitch are real and widely felt, and they are second-order relative to being wrong or incomplete. A review programme that traded correctness checking for concision checking would be trading down.

**The strongest single counterevidence is that this failure is not agent-specific, and that readers do not reliably punish it.** TensorFlow deliberately maintains separate "beginner" and "advanced" tutorials; a case study of 405 documentation-related Stack Overflow questions found a statistically significant but slight readability gap (Flesch Reading Ease 58.21 vs 54.28, p = 0.002), no significant difference in sentence count (p = 0.823) or word count (p = 0.616), and **no significant difference in the profiles — reputation, language-specific reputation, or v-index — of the developers asking about each tier** ([arXiv:2505.01939](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.01939)). Human authors, with an explicit two-tier audience model and years of effort, produced two documents that differ measurably and matter negligibly. Separately, in a controlled study of 75 lawyers and law students, a ~250-word "Verbose" acknowledgement of debt was rated *more* correct than a deliberately brief one (4.67 vs 4.24) and better in language quality (4.39 vs 4.13), preferred side-by-side 34 times to 9 ([Harasta et al., arXiv:2407.06798v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.06798)). And in the three studies that put model-authored documentation in front of human judges, the model won: RepoAgent recorded 70% and 91.33% win rates against human-authored documentation ([arXiv:2402.16667](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16667)), and Dvivedi et al. report that "barring Starchat, all LLMs consistently outperform the original documentation" on a checklist covering accuracy, completeness, relevance, understandability and readability ([arXiv:2312.10349](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10349)).

**The reconciliation, and the load-bearing claim of this report, is that reader preference is the wrong measurement instrument for reader fitness.** Every one of those favourable results is a preference or checklist judgement made by someone who is not performing the reader's task. Preference judgements are the same signal that produced length bias and typicality bias in the first place; a reader who prefers the longer document is exhibiting the bias, not refuting it. Task completion is the quantity that matters, and it is the quantity none of the favourable studies measured. Nor is it cheaply measurable: across eleven studies of usability evaluation, "[t]he average agreement between any two evaluators who have evaluated the same system using the same UEM ranges from 5% to 65%" ([Hertzum & Jacobsen 2003](https://forskning.ruc.dk/en/publications/the-evaluator-effect-a-chilling-fact-about-usability-evaluation-m)). **So the honest answer to this topic's question is: agent-authored documentation becomes generic, verbose and mispitched because the reader's state is unobservable to the author and the available quality signals reward the artifact that ignores it — and because the one measurement that would catch it is expensive, low-agreement, and almost never run.**

## Question, scope, and method

### Subquestions

- What distinguishable failure classes make up "generic, verbose, incorrectly pitched, or disconnected from tasks," and are they one failure or several?
- Which of these originate in what the agent cannot observe, and which in what the agent is optimized toward?

- How much do these failures actually cost readers, relative to correctness and completeness failures?

### Evidence and source plan

I inspected this repository directly, because the question turns on whether a document's intended reader is knowable, and this repository has a machine-readable answer to that in the form of a required `audiences` field. All local observations are at local commit `062c68741b07b969cce977632c6b4ea6a8323d53`, branch `topic-11-reader-task-fitness`, 2026-09-09, with commands reproduced inline. This worktree is a sparse checkout — 621 files on disk against 6,320 tracked — so every repository probe reads git objects (`git ls-tree`/`git show`/`git grep … HEAD`) rather than the working tree. No secrets or private host details are reproduced; the probes read front-matter keys and file sizes, all already public in a public repository.

Source priority ran: standards and first-party normative guidance (ISO/IEC/IEEE 26514:2022, Google's technical-writing course, Anthropic's context-engineering guidance) and peer-reviewed empirical software engineering (IEEE Software, ICSE, ICLR, EMNLP Findings, *Science Advances*, IJHCI) first; then arXiv preprints, marked as such at the point of use; then, for one figure I could not obtain from the paywalled primary, an institutional press release, explicitly labelled. Two sources are read at abstract level only (Dvivedi et al.; the zero-shot readability-control study) and are flagged where used.

Triangulation was applied to the two load-bearing mechanism claims. *Preference optimization rewards length* is supported independently by Singhal et al. (training-side, reward-model ablation), Dubois et al. (evaluation-side, regression control against an independent human arena), and Zheng et al.'s naming of verbosity bias as a systematic LLM-judge limitation ([arXiv:2306.05685](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.05685)) — three different teams, three different measurement positions. *Preference optimization rewards the typical* is supported independently by the Verbalized Sampling authors' data-level attribution, by Kirk et al.'s SFT-vs-RLHF diversity comparison, by Padmakumar and He's controlled writing experiment ([ICLR 2024, arXiv:2309.05196](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05196)), and by Doshi and Hauser's story-writing experiment ([*Science Advances* 2024](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290)). I treated the last two as evidence about *human-plus-model writing*, which is what documentation authoring in this repository actually is.

Counterevidence was sought deliberately and changed the shape of the answer three times. Uddin and Robillard's severity ranking demoted presentation problems below content problems, which forced the executive answer to state fitness failures as second-order. The TensorFlow case study showed the failure occurring in careful human-authored documentation with an explicit two-tier audience model, which forced the report to abandon any claim that this is agent-specific. The lawyer-preference and RepoAgent results showed readers preferring the artifact this report calls defective, which forced the central methodological claim — that preference is the wrong instrument — to be stated explicitly rather than assumed. I also searched for evidence that audience-persona prompting fixes the pitch problem and found a null result ([Zheng et al., Findings of EMNLP 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.888/)), which is carried into *Mitigations*.

Stopping condition: reached on source saturation. Further searching returned further instances of the same mechanism classes — length reward, typicality collapse, audience-declaration without audience-verification, task-orientation deficits in API documentation — without producing a new failure class, a new counterexample, or a change in confidence. Every subquestion has a supported answer or an explicit gap recorded in *Limits and open questions*.

## Causes and mechanisms

### Sourced: fitness is a difference, and one operand is not in the repository

Google's technical-writing course states the target as an explicit subtraction: "good documentation = knowledge and skills your audience needs to do a task − your audience's current knowledge and skills," and warns that "[e]xperts often suffer from **the curse of knowledge**, which means that their expert understanding of a topic ruins their explanations to newcomers" ([Google, *Technical Writing One*](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/audience)). ISO/IEC/IEEE 26514:2022 makes the same subtrahend a process obligation rather than a stylistic preference: the standard "describes how to establish what information users need, how to determine the way in which that information should be presented," and applies to "usability specialists and business analysts who identify the tasks that the intended users can perform with the software" ([IEEE SA](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/26514/7467/); published 2022-01-21).

**Interpretation.** A documentation agent working from repository evidence has excellent access to the minuend and none to the subtrahend. Source, schemas, tests and history describe what the system requires. They do not describe what the reader knows, what the reader is trying to do today, or which decision the reader is currently unable to make. Where the subtrahend cannot be estimated, the safe output is the one that assumes it is zero — a complete, self-contained account addressed to a reader with no prior knowledge and no particular task. That artifact is precisely generic, verbose, level-neutral and structure-organized. **The failure this topic names is not a mistake in the agent's reasoning; it is the correct output of a computation missing an operand.** This is my central interpretation and the rest of the mechanism section is subordinate to it.

The corollary matters for detection: a mispitched document contains no defect an agent could find by rereading it. Every sentence can be true, sourced and relevant to the system. The defect exists only in relation to a reader who is not represented in the artifact.

### Sourced: length is what preference optimization actually rewards

Singhal et al. report that in RLHF they find "improvements in reward to largely be driven by increasing response length, instead of other features," that reward models are "non-robust and easily influenced by length biases in preference data," and — the decisive ablation — that "even a purely length-based reward reproduces most downstream RLHF improvements over supervised fine-tuned models" ([arXiv:2310.03716](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03716)). Dubois et al. establish the same confound from the evaluation side: AlpacaEval "is known to favor models that generate longer outputs," and regressing out length raises Spearman correlation with the LMSYS Chatbot Arena from 0.94 to 0.98 ([arXiv:2404.04475](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.04475), submitted 2024-04-06, revised 2025-03-10). Zheng et al. name verbosity bias — an LLM judge favouring longer responses regardless of quality — as one of the systematic limitations of LLM-as-a-judge ([arXiv:2306.05685](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.05685), NeurIPS 2023).

**Interpretation.** These three results are measured at three different points in the pipeline — reward model, policy evaluation, and automated judging — and they agree. Two consequences follow for documentation. First, length is the default and concision is the intervention; an agent asked to document something will, absent a countervailing constraint, produce more text than the task requires. Second, and worse for review design, **the reviewer that would most naturally be delegated the concision check — an LLM judge — carries the same bias as the author.** A pipeline that generates with a preference-tuned model and reviews with a preference-tuned judge has no member that penalizes length.

### Sourced: typicality is what preference optimization also rewards

The Verbalized Sampling authors, unlike prior work attributing mode collapse to algorithmic limitations, "identify a fundamental, pervasive data-level driver: typicality bias in preference data, whereby annotators systematically favor familiar text as a result of well-established findings in cognitive psychology" ([arXiv:2510.01171](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01171), October 2025; preprint). Kirk et al. find that RLHF generalizes better than SFT under distribution shift but "substantially reduces output diversity," both per-input and across-input ([ICLR 2024](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/5a68d05006d5b05dd9463dd9c0219db0-Paper-Conference.pdf)). Padmakumar and He's controlled experiment found that writing argumentative essays with InstructGPT — but not with base GPT-3 — "results in a statistically significant reduction in diversity," increasing similarity between different authors' essays, with the effect "mainly attributable to InstructGPT contributing less diverse text to co-written essays" while "the user-contributed text remains unaffected" ([arXiv:2309.05196](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05196), ICLR 2024). Doshi and Hauser found that access to generative-AI story ideas raised individual novelty and usefulness ratings while raising inter-story similarity ([*Science Advances* 2024, DOI 10.1126/sciadv.adn5290](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290)); the publisher page returned HTTP 403 to my fetch, so the specific magnitudes I have — 300 writers and 600 evaluators, +8.1% novelty and +9% usefulness with maximum AI access, and "a 10.7% increase in similarity between writers whose stories used one generative AI-idea, compared with the group that didn't use AI" — come from the UCL press release ([EurekAlert, 2024-07-12](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1050888)) and are therefore secondary, though the abstract-level direction is confirmed by the DOI landing page's title and by the Dryad deposit.

**Interpretation.** Genericness in documentation is the same phenomenon these four sources measure, applied to a genre where the modal output has a specific and recognizable shape: an overview paragraph, a bulleted feature list, a configuration table, a troubleshooting section. Padmakumar and He's decomposition is the most useful of the four for this topic, because it locates the homogenization in the model-contributed spans specifically — which is exactly the substitution a documentation agent performs. **A caveat I consider material: none of these studies measures grounded technical writing.** In evidence-grounded documentation the content is pinned by the repository, so there is less room for typicality to operate on facts. My reading is that it operates instead on *framing* — which facts are foregrounded, what the section headings are, what the document assumes the reader is doing — and that this is exactly where fitness lives. I hold that reading at moderate confidence; I found no study that tests it.

### Sourced: the optimization target is the requester, not the reader

Sharma et al. find, using Bayesian logistic regression over human preference judgements, that matching a user's views "is one of the most predictive features of human preference judgments," and that "sycophantic responses are ranked more highly than non-sycophantic responses in existing human preference comparison data" ([arXiv:2310.13548](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548), Anthropic, October 2023).

**Interpretation.** In almost every documentation task the person issuing the request is not the person who will read the result. The requester is typically a maintainer who already knows the system; the reader is typically someone who does not. Sycophancy therefore does not merely bias tone — it points the residual optimization pressure at the wrong reader, and the wrong reader is precisely the one for whom a comprehensive, technically dense, assumption-laden document reads as good. This is my interpretation; I found no study of sycophancy in a documentation setting with a third-party reader, and I record that as a gap.

### Sourced: explicit pitch instructions do not reliably produce pitch

If the subtrahend cannot be observed, the obvious remedy is to supply it in the prompt. Two lines of evidence say this works less well than expected. On readability control, a zero-shot evaluation reports that "all tested models struggle to simplify sentences (especially to the lowest levels) due to models' limitations and characteristics of the source sentences that impede adequate rewriting," and additionally that standard automatic metrics "frequently misinterpreted typical simplification operations" — so both the control and its measurement are unreliable ([arXiv:2409.20246](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.20246); read at abstract level). On personas, Zheng, Pei, Logeswaran, Lee and Jurgens find that "adding personas in system prompts does not improve model performance across a range of questions compared to the control setting where no persona is added," and that while aggregating the best persona per question helps, "automatically identifying the best persona is challenging, with predictions often performing no better than random selection" ([Findings of EMNLP 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.888/)).

**Boundary condition, stated plainly:** the persona study measures factual accuracy on question answering, not the register or task-fitness of a written artifact, and the readability study measures sentence simplification, not documentation. Neither is decisive against audience-persona prompting for documentation. What they jointly establish is narrower and still useful: **an instruction naming an audience is not self-verifying, and the field's attempts to make models hit a stated level have a poor track record even on tasks far simpler than pitching a runbook.** Treating "I told it who the reader was" as evidence that the reader was served is not supported.

### Repository-grounded: audience is a declared field, not a verified property

Closed enum, not free text". done | sort -n | uniq -c

Per-value totals across those files are `agent` 356, `developer` 311, `reviewer` 271, `operator` 138 (plus a single `stakeholder`, outside the closed enum — a consistency defect belonging to topics 14 and 19, noted here only because I encountered it).

The corpus documented the ambiguity before the drift. `launchpad/docs/corpus/README.md` argues for excluding `agent` from its own `audiences` because "[t]he schema defines that field as who the node is written *for*", so listing `agent` "would make an audience-filtered view return two nodes to a cold-start agent, one of which only redirects to the other." It records the exclusion as, at that revision, "the corpus's first `developer` and its first deliberate audience *exclusion*", and states the competing convention explicitly: "A reader who thinks `audiences` should name everyone who might read a node rather than everyone it addresses would add `agent` back."

**Interpretation.** The corpus-wide distribution shows which convention prevailed: 75.1% of nodes that declare the field name three or more of four possible readers, and 21.4% name every reader the enum permits. Under the "everyone who addresses" reading these numbers would be implausible; under the "everyone who might read" reading they are expected. The mechanism this exposes generalizes well beyond this repository: **an audience field that is cheap to satisfy and syntactically checkable will be satisfied syntactically.** Declaring an extra audience costs nothing and risks nothing; excluding one requires an argument, which the entry-point node had to write three paragraphs to make. Adding a required `audiences` field to a schema therefore does not produce audience-fitted documents; it produces documents with an `audiences` field. Nothing in the schema, the fixtures, or `scaffold.py` checks whether the prose serves the declared reader — and no schema could, since the property is a relation between the text and a reader who is not present.

### Repository-grounded, with a negative result I did not expect

Document length across the tracked Markdown corpus (1,070 regular-file Markdown blobs, 18,023,431 bytes) splits sharply by tree: `launchpad/` (935 files, cohort-authored, heavily agent-assisted) has a median of 13,637 bytes; the upstream trees (135 files) have a median of 5,870 bytes. That is a 2.3× median gap and looks like evidence of agent verbosity.

**It is not, and I record the disconfirmation rather than the headline.** The gap is genre, not authorship: `launchpad/` contains research reports, plans and runbooks that are long by design, while the upstream Markdown set contains many short NIP and package stubs and one 329,540-byte `CHANGELOG.md`. Controlling crudely for genre by comparing `README.md` files only:

```bash
git ls-tree -r -l HEAD | awk -F'\t' '$2 ~ /README\.md$/ { split($1,a," ");
  if ($2 ~ /^launchpad\//) {ls+=a[4]; ln++} else {us+=a[4]; un++} }
  END {printf "launchpad: %d files, mean %d bytes\nupstream:  %d files, mean %d bytes\n", ln, ls/ln, un, us/un}'
# launchpad: 26 files, mean 5634 bytes
# upstream:  33 files, mean 5209 bytes
```

An 8% difference on a like-for-like genre. **This repository provides no evidence that its agent-assisted documentation is more verbose than its human-authored documentation, and I looked for it deliberately.** Two readings are available and I cannot separate them here: the fork's authoring conventions may be effectively constraining length, or file-size comparison may simply be too blunt an instrument for a failure that is about fitness rather than volume. My own view is the latter is doing most of the work — the failure classes in the taxonomy above are mostly invisible to a byte count — and that is itself a finding for the detection section.

### Sourced: for a machine reader, the cost function differs but the direction is the same

356 of the 370 audience-declaring nodes in this corpus name `agent` as a reader, so the machine-reader case is not hypothetical here. Anthropic's context-engineering guidance states the objective as finding "the *smallest* *possible* set of high-signal tokens that maximize the likelihood of some desired outcome," notes that "as the number of tokens in the context window increases, the model's ability to accurately recall information from that context decreases," describes an "attention budget," and names the two failure modes around the "Goldilocks zone": hardcoded, brittle over-specification on one side, and on the other "vague, high-level guidance that fails to give the LLM concrete signals for desired outputs or falsely assumes shared context" ([Anthropic Engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)). Empirically, ShortenDoc reports 25–40% docstring compression "while preserving the quality of generated code," against roughly 10% for conventional compression methods before degradation ([arXiv:2410.22793](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.22793)).

**Interpretation.** The human-reader literature and the machine-reader literature agree on the sign and disagree on the reason: humans pay a scanning cost, models pay an attention-budget cost. That convergence is a point in favour of concision as a general documentation property. But two boundary conditions cut against overreading it. First, ShortenDoc's compression is measured against *code-generation quality*, which is a narrow downstream task; a docstring compressed to serve code generation may be worse for a human deciding whether to call the function at all. Second, Anthropic's "falsely assumes shared context" failure mode is the same failure this report attributes to the missing subtrahend, restated for a machine reader — which suggests the fix is not "shorter" but "sized to a named reader," and that these are different instructions with different failure modes.

## Detection and validation

### Readability metrics: cheap, and weakly connected to the thing being measured

The TensorFlow case study is the most instructive single data point, because it applied a readability metric to a genuine two-tier audience design and got a result that is statistically real and practically negligible: Flesch Reading Ease 58.21 (beginner) vs 54.28 (advanced), Mann–Whitney p = 0.002, with no significant difference in total sentences (p = 0.823) or words (p = 0.616), and a significant difference only in example count ([arXiv:2505.01939](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.01939)). Both figures sit in the same difficulty band. **What this establishes:** a readability formula can detect a difference in pitch between two corpora. **What it cannot establish:** whether either corpus is pitched correctly for anyone — the authors' own conclusion is that "current TensorFlow documentation does not effectively support its target users" despite the difference being present and significant. Readability formulas score word and sentence length; nothing in that construct sees whether a prerequisite was assumed, a decision was left unstated, or four audiences were addressed at once.

### Structural and length metrics: detect volume, not fitness

The local probe above is the demonstration. Byte counts separated genres cleanly and separated authorship not at all. **What length metrics establish:** whether a corpus is growing, whether one document is an outlier for its genre, and — where a per-genre baseline exists — whether a specific document is unusually long for its kind. **What they cannot establish:** any of the taxonomy's classes except bloat, and not even bloat reliably, since a long reference page and a bloated tutorial have the same byte count.

The audience-count probe is a better instrument of the same cheap kind, because it measures a declaration the author actually made: a node declaring three or four of four audiences is a candidate for mode conflation or task disconnection. **What it cannot establish:** whether the node is in fact mispitched. It is a triage signal, not a finding.

### Preference tests: measure the wrong quantity, and are confounded

This is the sharpest methodological result in the report. Three studies put model-authored documentation in front of human judges and the model won each time: RepoAgent recorded win rates of 70% (105 vs 45) on Transformers and 91.33% (137 vs 13) on LlamaIndex, using three human evaluators judging 50 documentation pairs each, and the authors themselves acknowledge the absence of "benchmarks and datasets of exemplary human documentation," the subjectivity of the judgement, and the lack of "a unified quantitative evaluation method" ([arXiv:2402.16667](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16667)). Dvivedi et al., using a checklist over accuracy, completeness, relevance, understandability and readability, report that "barring Starchat, all LLMs consistently outperform the original documentation" ([arXiv:2312.10349](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10349), AIware 2024; read at abstract level). A large evaluation of AI-generated source-code comments reports 58.8% of generated comments rated fully equivalent to the original and 13.5% at the lowest two scores, with a further 27.7% reported as an improvement ([arXiv:2408.14007](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.14007); I read the HTML preprint and the labelling at the top of the scale is ambiguous, so I use the 58.8% and 13.5% figures and treat the third as approximate).

Three limits apply to all of them. **First, preference is the biased signal.** Length bias and typicality bias were *measured on human preference data*; a judge who prefers the longer, more familiar document is exhibiting the mechanism this report describes, not testing it. **Second, disclosed authorship confounds the judgement in both directions.** In a controlled experiment where 75 lawyers and law students evaluated two human-authored documents under swapped AI/human labels, mean correctness ratings were 4.69 when labelled human-crafted versus 4.21 when labelled AI-generated, Fisher exact p < 10⁻⁴ with Bonferroni correction for four comparisons ([Harasta et al., arXiv:2407.06798v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.06798)). Any blind comparison of agent- versus human-authored documentation is measuring something different from any labelled one. **Third, the judges are not doing the reader's task**, which is the quantity fitness is defined over.

### Task-based usability evaluation: the right instrument, with a low ceiling

Task-based evaluation — give a representative reader a representative task and the document, and observe completion — is the only method in this section that measures fitness directly. Its ceiling is set by the evaluator effect: across eleven studies of cognitive walkthrough, heuristic evaluation and thinking-aloud, "[t]he average agreement between any two evaluators who have evaluated the same system using the same UEM ranges from 5% to 65%," and the effect appears both in problem detection and in severity judgement ([Hertzum & Jacobsen 2003, *IJHCI* 15(1):183–204](https://forskning.ruc.dk/en/publications/the-evaluator-effect-a-chilling-fact-about-usability-evaluation-m)). RepoAgent's three-evaluator panel sits squarely inside that hazard.

**What task-based evaluation establishes:** that a specific reader could or could not complete a specific task with this document — the only direct evidence of fitness available. **What it cannot establish:** that the problems found are the important ones, that a second evaluator would find them, or that the result generalizes beyond the tasks chosen. Task selection is itself an audience assumption, so an evaluation designed by the document's author inherits the author's blind spot.

### Reader-behaviour mining: the strongest available proxy

The TensorFlow study's method — take 405 real documentation-related Stack Overflow questions, code them for what triggered each, and read the result as a fitness signal — produced the report's most actionable numbers: 64.30% of questions triggered by inadequate examples, 28.00% by ambiguity, and 24.9% concerning errors and exceptions. Aghajani et al.'s ICSE 2020 practitioner remedy points at the same idea from the other side: documentation "should be tested by someone with little domain knowledge," and specifically "if the documentation at hand is an installation guide, it should be tested by users of the system rather than a development team member."

**What this establishes:** where readers actually got stuck, on real tasks, without an evaluator designing the task. **What it cannot establish:** anything about readers who did not ask — questions that reach a support channel are, by construction, the failures, and silence is not evidence of fitness. It is also lagging: a document must be published and failing before it produces signal.

### The asymmetry that governs all of these

Correctness defects are properties of the artifact and can in principle be checked against the repository. Fitness defects are relations between the artifact and an absent reader, and cannot be checked against the repository at all. Every cheap method above therefore measures a proxy for the artifact side of the relation (length, readability, declared audience), and every method that measures the relation itself (task testing, behaviour mining) is expensive, lagging, or low-agreement. **This is why fitness failures survive review pipelines that catch factual ones, and it is a structural asymmetry rather than a gap in current tooling.**

## Mitigations

### Supported, with the strength of support stated

**Name one reader and one task per document.** This is the common recommendation of ISO/IEC/IEEE 26514:2022's process requirement to "establish what information users need," Google's knowledge-gap subtraction, Diátaxis's four-mode separation, and the action-oriented, task-anchored principles of Carroll and van der Meij's minimalism. Diátaxis states the reader-side rationale most sharply — a tutorial serves someone "at study" and a how-to guide serves someone "at work," and conflating them is "the single most common conflation made in software product documentation" ([diataxis.fr](https://diataxis.fr/tutorials-how-to/)). **Trade-off:** more documents, more cross-references, and more fragmentation risk — which is itself a reported reader complaint ("10s of clicks through links to find the information you need", R2:69 in Uddin & Robillard). Mode separation trades a tangling failure for a fragmentation failure; the ICSE 2020 population rated findability important at 65%, higher than superfluous content at 55%. **Support level:** normative and widely adopted, not experimentally isolated. I found no controlled study measuring task completion under mode-separated versus mode-conflated documentation.

**Derive the audience from evidence rather than asserting it.** The TensorFlow result is the argument: a two-tier audience *assertion* produced a 3.9-point readability difference and no measurable difference in who read each tier. Reader-behaviour mining — support questions, issue traffic, search queries — identifies the audience from what actually happened. **Trade-off:** lagging, and biased toward readers who complain in public. **Boundary:** unavailable for a new document, which is where pitch decisions are actually made.

**Constrain length against a per-genre baseline rather than an absolute one.** The local probe shows why: the same corpus contains 5.6 KB READMEs and 130 KB plans, and a single threshold would be wrong for both. **Trade-off:** a length constraint is a proxy that can be satisfied by deleting the wrong content; ShortenDoc's 25–40% compression figure is measured against code-generation quality specifically ([arXiv:2410.22793](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.22793)), and does not license the same compression for a human deciding whether to use an API at all.

**Have the document tested by someone without the author's domain knowledge.** This is the practitioner-generated remedy in Aghajani et al.'s ICSE 2020 survey, and it directly attacks the curse of knowledge that Google names. **Trade-off:** it is the expensive method, and the evaluator effect bounds what one tester's report is worth (5–65% agreement between any two evaluators). **Interpretation:** its value is asymmetric — a naive tester who *fails* has produced a real finding, while one who succeeds has established very little.

### Where mitigations fail, backfire, or have no measured effect

**Audience-persona prompting has a published null result on the nearest measured task.** "[A]dding personas in system prompts does not improve model performance across a range of questions compared to the control setting," and automatic best-persona selection performs "no better than random selection" ([Zheng et al., Findings of EMNLP 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.888/)). The transfer to documentation pitch is not established — that study measures QA accuracy — but the burden of proof sits with anyone claiming persona prompting fixes pitch, and I found no study discharging it.

**Explicit level targets are not reliably hit.** Models "struggle to simplify sentences (especially to the lowest levels)", and the automatic metrics used to check the result "frequently misinterpreted typical simplification operations" ([arXiv:2409.20246](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.20246); abstract-level reading). Both the control and the verification are weak.

**A required audience field produces declarations, not fitness.** This is the report's clearest repository-grounded mitigation limit: under a schema that requires `audiences`, validates its members against a closed enum, and rejects duplicates, 75.1% of declaring nodes name three or more of four audiences and 21.4% name all four. Declaring an audience is cheap; excluding one requires an argument. A schema field is a triage signal at best, and adopting one should not be mistaken for adopting the practice it names.

**An LLM reviewer shares the author's biases.** Verbosity bias is a named, measured property of LLM judges ([Zheng et al., arXiv:2306.05685](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.05685)), and typicality bias sits in the preference data that trained both ([arXiv:2510.01171](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01171)). Delegating the concision and genericness check to a model of the same family is delegating it to a party with the same defect.

**Concision may not be what readers want.** In the one controlled study I found that manipulated verbosity directly, the verbose document won on every measure: correctness 4.67 vs 4.24, language quality 4.39 vs 4.13, preferred side-by-side 34 times to 9 with 32 ties, among 75 legal professionals and students ([Harasta et al., arXiv:2407.06798v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.06798)). The authors note the document comparison "is not the subject of this study," the domain is legal drafting rather than software documentation, and the "Verbose" document was only ~250 words — so this is a bounded result, not a refutation. **But it is the correct caution to carry:** a concision mitigation validated by reader preference may well validate as harmful, and that would not mean it was wrong.

### Counterevidence carried rather than resolved

Uddin and Robillard's severity data stands against any strong prioritization of fitness work: every problem their 254-respondent validation survey ever rated a "Blocker" — incompleteness, ambiguity, obsoleteness, incorrectness, inconsistency, unexplained examples — is a content problem, and no respondent selected tangled information or excessive structural information as a top problem to fix. Their finding of "no statistically significant evidence that novices experienced documentation problems differently" also cuts against expertise-tiered documentation as a general remedy, at least on the problem-experience measure they used. And the TensorFlow result establishes that this failure class is not agent-specific: careful human authors with an explicit audience model produced documentation the authors conclude "does not effectively support its target users."

## Limits and open questions

- **No study measures reader or task fitness of agent-authored repository documentation.** Every quantitative figure in this report is transferred: from human-authored API documentation (Uddin & Robillard; Aghajani et al.; the TensorFlow study), from open-ended generation (the mode-collapse and length-bias literature), from legal drafting (Harasta et al.), or from usability methodology (Hertzum & Jacobsen). The directions are supported and, in my judgement, robust. The magnitudes are not transferable and I have not treated them as such.
- **The mechanism evidence and the harm evidence do not overlap.** Length bias, typicality bias and sycophancy are measured on chat-style open generation; bloat, tangling and task disconnection are measured on human-authored technical documentation. The claim that the first set causes the second set in agent-authored documentation is my central interpretation and is untested. The specific untested sub-claim I would most want checked: that in evidence-grounded writing, typicality bias operates on *framing and organization* rather than on facts.
- **The favourable studies are not merely weak; they may be measuring a different construct.** RepoAgent's three evaluators, Dvivedi et al.'s checklist, and the code-comment study's equivalence scoring all ask "is this good documentation?" rather than "could a reader do the task?" I have argued that preference is the wrong instrument. That argument is an interpretation, and a reader who thinks preference is a legitimate proxy for fitness should read this report's conclusion as considerably weaker.
- **The local probes measure declaration, not reading.** I can show that 75.1% of audience-declaring nodes name three or more audiences. I cannot show that any of them failed a reader, because this repository records no reading outcome — no documentation-triggered issue traffic, no search logs, no task tests. The negative length result (READMEs at parity between trees) is real and I have reported it as a disconfirmation, but it is equally consistent with byte count being the wrong measure.
- **The machine-reader case is under-evidenced and locally dominant.** 356 of 370 audience-declaring nodes here name `agent`. The entire reader-harm literature I have cited concerns humans. Anthropic's context-engineering guidance and ShortenDoc point the same direction as the human literature, but a genuinely agent-first corpus may have a different optimum — for instance, redundancy that wastes a human's scan may be cheap insurance against a retrieval miss. I found no empirical work resolving this, and it is the largest open question for this repository specifically.
- **`stakeholder` appears once as an audience value outside the schema's closed enum.** I encountered this while probing and record it for completeness; diagnosing it belongs to topics 14 and 19, not here.
- **Two sources are read at abstract level** (Dvivedi et al.; the zero-shot readability-control study) and **one magnitude is taken from an institutional press release** rather than the paywalled paper (Doshi & Hauser). Each is flagged at the point of use. One primary PDF (Uddin & Robillard) and two others were text-extracted locally from the publisher's own PDF; quotations are from that extraction.
- **Open question I could not answer:** whether any measurable artifact property correlates with task-completion outcomes for technical documentation. If one existed, fitness review would become cheap. Readability formulas, length, and declared-audience count all failed to be that property in the evidence surveyed here, and I found no candidate that succeeded.

## Practical review checks

Stated as research output for later evaluation, not as adopted policy. Each is paired with what it cannot establish.

- **Audience-count triage.** Flag documents declaring three or more audiences from a closed enum for pitch review. *Cannot establish* that the document is mispitched — only that it declared no pitch. Locally this would flag 278 of 370 nodes, so it is a prioritization signal that needs a second filter to be useful.
- **Single-decision test.** Ask what decision the intended reader is trying to make and whether the document states which option to take, when, and what happens if the reader takes the wrong one. *Cannot establish* fitness for a reader with a different decision; it tests one task at a time by design.
- **Mode audit.** Check whether one document is simultaneously teaching, instructing, listing and explaining. *Cannot establish* harm — mode separation is normatively supported and experimentally unvalidated, and separating a document trades tangling risk for fragmentation risk.
- **Restatement check.** Flag passages that restate what the reader's tools already show (signatures, hierarchies, field lists). *Cannot establish* priority — no respondent in the 254-person IBM validation survey selected excess structural information as a top problem.
- **Per-genre length outlier check.** Compare against a baseline for the document's own genre, never an absolute threshold. *Cannot establish* whether the excess text is the unnecessary part.
- **Naive-reader trial on procedures.** For any installation, deployment or recovery procedure, have someone without the author's domain knowledge attempt it. *Cannot establish* fitness if they succeed; failure is the informative outcome, and one evaluator's findings carry the 5–65% agreement caveat.
- **Reader-behaviour reconciliation.** Where support questions, issue traffic or search queries exist, code what triggered them and compare against what the documentation assumed. *Cannot establish* anything about readers who did not ask.
- **Do not delegate the concision or genericness check to an LLM judge alone.** Verbosity bias in LLM judges is measured, and typicality bias sits in the preference data behind both author and judge. *Cannot establish* that a human reviewer is unbiased either — human preference data is where both biases were measured.
- **Record the instrument, not just the verdict, when evaluating agent-authored documentation.** Blind and disclosed comparisons measure different things (4.69 vs 4.21 on identical documents under swapped labels). *Cannot establish* which framing is correct; it establishes that an unstated one makes the result uninterpretable.
