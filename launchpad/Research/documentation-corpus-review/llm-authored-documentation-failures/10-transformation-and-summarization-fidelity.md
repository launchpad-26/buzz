## Executive answer

**The five harms named in the question are not five independent defects. They are what happens when a transformation changes an artifact's evidence relationship to its source while leaving its surface intact.** Every operation in the question — summarize, rewrite, split, merge, migrate, restructure — replaces a document whose claims were checked against something with a document whose claims are checked against the *previous document*. The prose survives the operation; the grounding does not. That framing is my interpretation. The mechanisms under it are separately sourced, and they are unusually well measured for a topic this diffuse.

**What is lost is disproportionately the material that bounded the claim.** Expert annotation of GPT-3 biomedical summaries found omissions more common than outright errors, with errors typically minor ([Shaib et al., ACL 2023](https://aclanthology.org/2023.acl-short.119/)). What gets dropped is not random: across 4,900 summaries from 10 models, most models stated results more broadly than their sources, and did so in 26–73% of cases for the worst performers, at odds ratio 4.85 against expert-written human summaries of the same material ([Peters & Chin-Yee, *Royal Society Open Science*, April 2025](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.241776)). Scope conditions, populations and preconditions are low-salience and high-consequence; compression selects against exactly that class.

**What is altered most reliably is certainty.** A June 2026 preprint measuring rewriting specifically — paraphrase, simplification, summarization, over scientific, news and radiology text — reports certainty distortion in up to 75% of outputs, with inflation outnumbering deflation by 1.5–2×, no improvement with model scale, and compounding across iterations in the medical domain ([Belem et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.07951v1)). For documentation this is the failure that matters most, and it is invisible to every check that asks "is this claim supported?": *may* rewritten as *is* remains topically supported while ceasing to be true.

**Splitting and merging fail for a reason that has nothing to do with generation quality.** Zhang, Wan & Bansal annotated 1,600 summaries from 16 *extractive* systems — systems that only copy sentences and write nothing — and found 30% carried at least one unfaithfulness problem, principally broken coreference and broken discourse ([ACL 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.03549)). Interpretation: moving prose is not a safe operation. A sentence's truth depends on antecedents that a split leaves behind, and this is confirmed from the engineering side — Anthropic reports that re-supplying chunk context cut retrieval failure by 35% on its own and 49% combined with contextual BM25 ([2024-09-19](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval)). Merging fails symmetrically: analysis of five abstractive systems found fused sentences "mostly grammatical, but often fail to remain faithful" ([Lebanoff et al. 2019](https://aclanthology.org/D19-5413/)) — the merge produces exactly the artifact a reviewer is least equipped to doubt.

**False reconciliation is the one harm with a specific, load-bearing local demonstration, and it inverts the usual advice.** Merging is where an agent must decide between disagreeing sources, and models resolve that in favour of the retrieved text: ClashEval found models abandon their own correct prior for incorrect retrieved content over 60% of the time ([Wu, Wu & Zou, NeurIPS 2024 D&B](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10198)). In this repository the corresponding failure is reproducible at HEAD. On 2026-08-14 a documentation-consistency fix rewrote `launchpad/README.md`'s correct "one approving review" to match `launchpad/AGENTS.md`'s "two", and shipped a script to keep them agreeing (commit `aa025924c`). On 2026-08-28 `AGENTS.md` was corrected against the branch-protection API to "**one** approving review is required — not two", noting that the "two" figure "came from a merge box read on 2026-08-13 and was already wrong" (commit `87abbed79`). At `5f675645` the README still says two, and the guard now fails its own third assertion. **Consistency enforcement propagated an error into the document that had it right, and the check written to prevent recurrence measures agreement, not truth.**

**Homogenization is the harm with the weakest direct evidence and I do not want to overstate it.** It is measured for creative and argumentative writing — AI-assisted stories are more similar to one another ([Doshi & Hauser, *Science Advances* 2024](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290)); writing with a feedback-tuned model significantly reduces inter-author diversity ([Padmakumar & He, ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05196)) — and is corroborated for chained rewriting, where distortion accumulates and textual properties converge ([Mohamed et al., ACL 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.371/)). No study I found measures it on a documentation corpus. The transfer is interpretation, and it is the weakest link in this report.

**The material limitation on all of the above:** none of it was measured on technical documentation. The load-bearing counterevidence is that LLM summaries are judged *on par with* human-written ones on holistic quality ([Zhang et al., TACL 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.tacl-1.3/)) — which does not contradict the fidelity findings so much as explain why they survive review. Quality judgement and fidelity are different objects, and the transformations here are precisely the ones that improve the first while degrading the second.

## Question, scope, and method

### Subquestions

- What distinguishable classes of information are lost, altered, generalized, falsely reconciled or homogenized by each of the six named operations?
- Which harms are properties of *compression* specifically, and which arise from *relocation* of text that is not compressed at all?
- What happens at a merge when two sources disagree, and how does an agent resolve it?
- Does harm accumulate when transformations are chained, or does it plateau?
- Which harms are detectable by existing automated methods, and which are structurally invisible to them?
- What mitigations are supported, and where do they backfire or trade one harm for another?
- Where is the counterevidence — when is transformation neutral or beneficial, and what does that imply about review?

### Exclusions

- **Context selection and evidence sufficiency before authoring** — topic 01. This report begins after the source material is in hand.
- **Tool-mediated evidence failures** (truncation, search misses) — topic 02, except where a chunking boundary is itself the transformation under study.
- **Temporal coherence across incompatible revisions** — topic 03. Staleness appears here only as one input to a false reconciliation.
- **Hallucination and unsupported inference from a fixed context** — topic 04. Fabrication introduced *by* a transformation is in scope; fabrication in original authoring is not.
- **Citation provenance and circularity** — topic 05, though the severance of an evidence anchor during restructuring is treated here as a transformation defect and marked at that boundary.
- **Omission in original authoring and false completeness** — topic 07. Omission *relative to a specific source document being transformed* is in scope; corpus-level coverage is topic 16.
- **Normative distortion as a standing failure class** — topic 09. Certainty and modality change *caused by a rewrite* is in scope here and is the boundary between the two reports.
- **Cross-document terminology drift from repeated independent edits** — topic 14; **error propagation into later agent context** — topic 15; **reviewer psychology** — topic 18; **breadth of automated detection** — topic 19.
- Machine translation of documentation between natural languages, and format conversion (Markdown → HTML) where no editorial decision is taken.

### Evidence and source plan

I inspected this repository directly, because it contains three things that are unusually good evidence for this topic: a documented, still-live false reconciliation with its full commit history; a normative standard governing exactly the split/merge decision (`launchpad/docs/corpus/standards/atomicity.md`); and a deterministic corpus validator whose relationship check I could read to establish what a structural check can and cannot see. All local observations were taken at local commit `5f675645565e1ac30033f952571b5b03df8e7abb`, branch `topic-10-transformation-fidelity`, on 2026-09-09, with commands reproduced inline. No secrets, credentials or private host details are reproduced.

Source priority was: peer-reviewed venues (ACL, NAACL, TACL, ICLR, NeurIPS, *Science Advances*, *Royal Society Open Science*) first; then first-party engineering reports, which are authoritative for how splitting is actually done in production systems; then preprints, each marked. Where a publisher paywalled the article (`royalsocietypublishing.org` returned HTTP 403 on 2026-09-09) I read the open-access arXiv and PMC renderings and say so against the claim.

Triangulation was applied to the three load-bearing claims. *Compression preferentially removes scope conditions* is supported independently by Peters & Chin-Yee's generalization coding and by Shaib et al.'s expert annotation of a different genre, with a third, mechanistically distinct line in Belem et al.'s certainty measurement. *Relocation alone breaks fidelity* is supported by Zhang et al.'s extractive annotation (research) and by Anthropic's contextual-retrieval measurement (engineering) — two different fields, different methods, same conclusion. *Merging resolves conflict rather than reporting it* rests on ClashEval, on Shaib et al.'s multi-document result, and on the local case; those are three independent chains, but only the first is a controlled measurement.

Counterevidence was sought deliberately and changed two conclusions. Zhang et al.'s TACL result that LLM summaries are judged on par with human ones is carried into the report as a reconciliation problem rather than discarded. Ladhak et al.'s faithfulness–abstractiveness trade-off is carried into *Mitigations*, because it shows that the most reliable fidelity mitigation works by cancelling the transformation. I also looked for, and did not find, evidence that documentation-genre transformation is *safer* than news or biomedical transformation; absence of that evidence is recorded as a gap, not as reassurance.

Stopping condition: reached on source saturation for the mechanisms. Further searching returned additional instances of the same classes (more faithfulness metrics, more simplification studies, more diversity experiments) without producing a new failure class, a new counterexample, or a change in confidence. It did *not* close the genre gap: the documentation-specific measurement does not appear to exist, and that is recorded as the report's principal limit rather than papered over.

## Failure taxonomy

Classes are organised by *what the transformation did to the text*, because that determines which check could catch it. Class names are mine; the evidence column is sourced.

| Failure class | What fails | Evidence and boundary conditions |
|---|---|---|
| **T1. Silent omission under compression** | Detail present in the source is absent from the output, and the output reads complete. | Expert annotation of GPT-3 biomedical summaries: "omissions were more common than errors; errors were typically minor" ([Shaib et al., ACL 2023](https://aclanthology.org/2023.acl-short.119/); full text read via [PMC11613457](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11613457/), 2026-09-08). Boundary: biomedical abstracts, 100 articles × 3 expert annotators; genre transfer is interpretation. |
| **T2. Loss of scope conditions → overgeneralization** | Population, preconditions, sample and qualifiers drop out, converting a bounded finding into a general rule. | 10 models, 4,900 summaries; worst performers overgeneralized in 26–73% of cases; OR = 4.85 (95% CI [3.06, 7.70]) against expert human summaries ([Peters & Chin-Yee 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.00025)). Coded categories: generic present-tense claims without quantifiers, present-tense framing, and descriptive→prescriptive conversion. Boundary: scientific abstracts, not documentation; publisher page 403 on 2026-09-09, figures read from arXiv and [PMC12042776](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12042776/). |

| **T5. Boundary: news; but this is the strongest available evidence that the harm is not a generation artefact — no generation occurred. |
| **T6. Reconciliation toward the wrong value** | A consistency repair propagates the error *into* the correct document, and a guard locks it in. | Local, reproducible (below): commits `aa025924c` (2026-08-14) and `87abbed79` (2026-08-28) in this repository; the guard `launchpad/scripts/test-readme-review-count.sh` fails its own third assertion at `5f675645`. Boundary: one claim, one repository — an existence proof of the mechanism, not a rate. |

Corroborated incidentally: over 70% of GPT-3's multi-document summaries had at least a quarter of their sentences copied from the input ([Shaib et al. 2023, via PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11613457/)). |

## Causes and mechanisms

### Sourced: compression selects by salience, and the bounding material is not salient

The generalization result is the cleanest evidence that loss is not random. Peters & Chin-Yee coded three specific linguistic conversions — a result stated as a generic present-tense claim without quantifiers, a past-tense finding reframed in the present, and a descriptive finding reframed as a recommendation — and found most of ten models performing them, with the worst at 26–73% and newer models performing *worse* than earlier ones ([2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.00025)). Each conversion deletes a different bound: the population, the tense that located the claim in a study, and the gap between "was observed" and "you should do".

Interpretation, offered as such: those three are precisely the moves a documentation summary is rewarded for. "In the configuration we tested, the relay reconnected within 30 s" is worse *as a summary* than "the relay reconnects within 30 s". The generic form is shorter, more useful-sounding, and more quotable, and it is false in exactly the cases a reader consults documentation to handle. I found no source measuring this on documentation; the mechanism transfers, the rate does not.

### Sourced: the artefact of a rewrite is not the same claim, and certainty is the axis that moves

Belem et al. isolate rewriting from summarization — paraphrase and simplification alongside summarization, on scientific claim pairs (SPICED, 397 pairs) and radiology reports (MIMIC-CXR, 800 sentences) — and find distortion in 37.5–69% of scientific paraphrases and 44.3–75.3% of news rewrites, with inflation dominating deflation by 1.5–2× and the largest model variants still at 39.5–70% ([2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.07951v1)). Two features of that result matter more than the headline. First, scale does not help, so this is not a capability gap that waits out. Second, the asymmetry is directional: models raise certainty in 50–75% of *hedged* source texts. Hedging is a signal that survives poorly under rewriting.

Interpretation: hedges in technical documentation are load-bearing in a way they are not in prose generally. "This usually works" and "this works" are different operational commitments, and the second is what an agent's rewrite tends to produce. This is the boundary with topic 09 (normative distortion): topic 09 owns invented and weakened obligations as a standing class; what this report contributes is that *the rewrite itself* is a measured cause.

### Sourced: relocation breaks text that nobody rewrote

This is the finding that reframes split, merge and restructure as fidelity operations rather than layout operations. Zhang, Wan & Bansal annotated only extractive output — sentences copied verbatim — and still found 30% of 1,600 summaries carrying at least one of five problems, all of them consequences of removing a sentence from its surroundings: incorrect coreference, incomplete coreference, incorrect discourse, incomplete discourse, and other misleading information ([ACL 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.03549)). No model wrote a false sentence. The falseness was created by the move.

The engineering literature reaches the same place from the other direction. Anthropic's chunking example — "The company's revenue grew by 3% over the previous quarter", with no company and no quarter — is the same defect, and their measurement is that re-attaching context to each chunk reduced retrieval failure from 5.7% to 3.7%, and to 2.9% when combined with contextual BM25 ([2024-09-19](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval)). That is a retrieval metric, not a correctness metric, and I do not read it as one; what it establishes is the size of the context that a naive split silently discards.

Interpretation: a documentation split has all of the exposure and none of the recovery. A retrieval system can re-supply context because it holds the parent document; a corpus that has been physically split into files has replaced the parent. The pronouns, the "as above", the "unless the previous step failed", and the implicit subject carried from a section heading are the antecedents at risk, and they are invisible in a diff that shows only that text moved.

### Sourced: a merge forces a resolution, and the resolution is not neutral

Merging is the only operation in the question that *requires* the agent to adjudicate. When sources disagree, ClashEval measures the direction of that adjudication: across 1,200+ questions in six domains with perturbations ranging from subtle to blatant, models abandoned their own correct prior in favour of incorrect retrieved content more than 60% of the time, and were more likely to do so the lower their token-probability confidence ([Wu, Wu & Zou, NeurIPS 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10198)). Shaib et al.'s multi-document result is the documentation-adjacent analogue: single-document summaries were faithful, aggregation over several was not, with over half of generated meta-analysis summaries disagreeing with the expert reference and a systematic tilt toward "marginal or substantive beneficial effects" ([2023, PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11613457/), read 2026-09-08).

Interpretation: nothing in a merge asks "do these agree?" The operation asks for one coherent output, and one coherent output is what fluency optimises for. A disagreement between two documents is information — often the single most valuable piece of information in the corpus, because it localises where the corpus is wrong — and merging is the operation that destroys it most efficiently.

### Sourced and reproducible: consistency machinery can propagate the error

The local case is worth setting out in full because it is the mechanism running to completion with a guard in place.

`launchpad/README.md` and `launchpad/AGENTS.md` both state how many approving reviews the protected `launchpad` branch requires. `README.md` originally said one. On 2026-08-14, commit `aa025924c` ("docs(launchpad): README says one approving review; two are enforced") rewrote it to match `AGENTS.md`:

$ git show aa025924c -- launchpad/README.md      # the diff hunk, elided of context lines
-need one approving review — the branch is protected and you can't approve your own.
+need two approving reviews from reviewers with write access — the branch is protected
+and you can't approve your own.

The commit message records the evidence used — a live PR's merge box, "the only place the count is readable without admin:org" — and adds `launchpad/scripts/test-readme-review-count.sh`, described in the same message as "a regression guard against the same drift happening again in the other direction."

On 2026-08-28, commit `87abbed79` corrected `AGENTS.md` against the branch-protection API. At `5f675645` it reads:

Running the guard against the tree at `5f675645`:

```

# two files it reads were extracted from HEAD into a scratch directory and run there:
$ git show HEAD:launchpad/scripts/test-readme-review-count.sh > probe/scripts/t.sh
$ git show HEAD:launchpad/README.md  > probe/README.md
$ git show HEAD:launchpad/AGENTS.md  > probe/AGENTS.md
$ bash probe/scripts/t.sh; echo "exit=$?"

PASS: README.md no longer says one approving review

```

Three observations, each verifiable at that commit. **First**, the reconciliation moved the correct document to the incorrect value: `README.md` said one, one was right, and it now says two. **Second**, the guard encodes the error as its expected state — its first assertion is `grep -q "two approving reviews" README.md` — so it cannot ever be the thing that detects this. **Third**, the guard does not run: at `5f675645` no file under `.github/workflows/` references it, and the only reference anywhere in the tree is an inventory row in `launchpad/Research/342-cohort-executable-inventory.md`. Interpretation: a consistency check written to prevent drift became a mechanism for preserving one specific wrong value, and then stopped running, and the corpus contains a live contradiction that a reader has no way to adjudicate from the documents themselves.

I want to be fair to the 2026-08-14 change. It was well reasoned, cited its evidence, and named its own weakness. The failure is structural, not careless: the reconciliation direction was chosen by *which document was more authoritative*, not by *which claim had better evidence*, and those came apart. This is the same asymmetry [Joren et al. (ICLR 2025)](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.06037v3) identify for retrieval — an output that looks right does not certify the evidence behind it — arriving through a different door.

### Sourced and local: the split/merge decision is not recoverable, and the two errors do not cost the same

This repository's corpus standard states the asymmetry directly, and it is the most useful framing I found anywhere for split/merge fidelity. From `launchpad/docs/corpus/standards/atomicity.md` at `5f675645`:

> **Over-merging fails silently.** Splitting a node later moves half its content to a new `id`. Every inbound edge that meant the departed half still resolves, because the checker confirms only that a target matches some loaded node's `id` — never that the target still covers what the edge was drawn for. The edges now point somewhere wrong and no run will ever say so.
>
> **Over-splitting fails visibly.** Merging two nodes later retires one by status change. The file stays, so inbound edges keep resolving; the retired node's body says what replaced it; the survivor declares `supersedes`. A reader arriving on an old edge is told where to go.

I verified the enabling fact first-hand rather than taking the node's word for it. `launchpad/project-intelligence/corpus/validate.py` defines `find_unresolved_relationship_targets`, whose docstring reads "Every `relationships[].target` must match some loaded node's `id`", and whose body checks `target not in known_ids`. There is no check that the target still covers the subject the edge was drawn for, and no such check is expressible from ids alone.

Interpretation: this generalises past this corpus. Any structural validator — link checkers, anchor checkers, cross-reference resolvers — verifies that a pointer resolves, and cannot verify that it resolves to the thing it was pointing at. Restructuring is the operation that changes what a resolving pointer means while leaving it resolving, which makes it the operation most thoroughly invisible to the checks a documentation pipeline usually has.

### Interpretation: why chaining is the compounding risk, and where it is not

Two sources bear on this and they disagree in a way that is informative. Mohamed et al. find distortion accumulating with chain length across iterative translation and rephrasing chains ([ACL 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.371/)). Belem et al. find certainty distortion *plateauing* after the first iteration on scientific tasks while compounding in the medical domain ([2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.07951v1)).

My reading is that these are compatible and the plateau is the more important finding for documentation. A plateau means the largest single loss happens at the **first** transformation, and that subsequent passes mostly hold the new position rather than continuing to drift. If that transfers, the reviewable moment is the first rewrite, and a corpus that has already been transformed once is not made much worse by being transformed again — but neither is it recoverable by further transformation. I have low confidence in this transfer: two studies, neither on documentation, disagreeing on the shape of the curve.

## Detection and validation

### What existing automated methods measure

**Claim-level faithfulness metrics measure precision and are structurally blind to omission.** This is the single most important detection limit for this topic, and it is a definitional consequence rather than a quality problem: entailment- and claim-verification-based metrics ask whether each stated claim is supported by the source, so a document can score near-perfectly by saying less. Santillana states it plainly — such metrics "share a blind spot: they measure only precision … and therefore reward abstention, since a model can score near-perfect faithfulness by saying almost nothing" ([arXiv 2606.09376, June 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09376), **single-author preprint**). Since T1 (silent omission) is the most common defect Shaib et al. observed, the most widely deployed class of check is aimed away from the most common failure.

**Coverage/recall checks can see omission but need an oracle of what mattered.** Santillana's proposal is a combined faithfulness-coverage score against a complete ground-truth oracle, achieving F1 ≈ 0.98 for fine-tuned small models on a telemetry benchmark where frontier models covered "under half of the relevant facts". For documentation there is no such oracle: the set of facts that mattered in the source document is exactly the judgement the transformation was supposed to make. Interpretation: coverage checking is feasible only where somebody first writes down what must survive — which converts a detection problem into an authoring problem, and is why the candidate checks below ask for that list up front.

**Structural validators verify resolution, not meaning.** Established first-hand above: `find_unresolved_relationship_targets` in this repository's validator confirms an id exists and nothing more. Link checkers, anchor checkers and id-uniqueness checks share this shape. They are cheap, deterministic and worth running; they cannot detect T10 at all.

**Consistency checks cannot establish correctness, and can entrench error.** Demonstrated reproducibly above. A check that two documents agree is satisfied by two documents that are identically wrong, and once written it makes the wrong value the expected state. I regard this as the most transferable negative result in the report, because cross-document consistency checking is the check a documentation corpus is most likely to actually have.

**Certainty-preservation checks exist but are noisy.** Belem et al.'s LM-judge, validated against 240 text pairs annotated by 129 human raters, reaches τ = 0.47 with human consensus ([2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.07951v1)). That beats the prior BERT-based approach (τ = 0.37) and is nowhere near reliable enough to gate on per-claim. Interpretation: usable as a triage signal over a batch of rewrites, not as a per-document verdict.

### What human review measures, and why it misses this

Two findings should be read together. Lebanoff et al. found fused sentences "mostly grammatical, but often fail to remain faithful" ([2019](https://aclanthology.org/D19-5413/)). Zhang et al. found LLM summaries "judged to be on par with human written summaries" on holistic quality ([TACL 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.tacl-1.3/)). Neither is a fidelity result: the first says fluency and fidelity dissociate, the second says holistic judgement tracks something other than fidelity well enough to reach parity.

Interpretation, and the practical consequence: a reviewer reading a transformed document *as a document* is measuring the wrong object, for the same reason report 01 gives for evidence sufficiency. The only review that can catch T1–T3 is a differential one — the source open beside the output, claim by claim — and that review costs roughly what re-doing the transformation costs. This is the boundary with topic 18, which owns reviewer psychology; what this report contributes is that the defect classes here are specifically the ones a fluent artefact conceals.

### What nothing here can establish

- **That a transformation preserved everything that mattered.** Every method above is a falsifier. Interpretation: the honest report of a clean check is "no loss detected against the claims we listed", never "faithful".
- **That a merge's reconciliation was correct.** Detecting that two sources disagreed is tractable; deciding which was right requires re-deriving from the underlying system, i.e. discarding both documents as evidence.
- **That a restructure preserved the meaning of its cross-references.** No id-based check can reach this, per the validator reading above.
- **Homogenization at corpus scale.** Diversity metrics exist for creative-writing experiments; I found nothing that measures whether a technical corpus lost genre distinctions, and I would not trust an off-the-shelf lexical-diversity metric to distinguish homogenization from a house style being applied correctly.

## Mitigations

**Prefer linking to transforming.** The strongest mitigation is to not perform the operation. This repository's atomicity standard states it as guidance B4 — "Link the node or task that owns an adjacent subject rather than summarising it" — and pairs it with the reason a summary is worse than a pointer: for claims about current behaviour, "a corpus node restating a volatile implementation detail adds a copy that can only fall behind its own source". Trade-off: pointers cost the reader a hop and fragment reading flow, which is exactly the pressure that produces merges.

**When a split/merge call is balanced, split.** Follows from the asymmetry established above: over-merging fails silently, over-splitting fails visibly (`atomicity.md`, guidance B2). Boundary condition, stated in the same standard: this is not a preference for small documents — requirement A5 forbids splitting merely to shorten and merging merely to reduce file count, and the standalone test vetoes a split whose halves cannot state their own claim.

**Record what a split declined and where it went.** Requirement A4, with the failure it prevents named: "A subject that quietly became two nodes, with neither saying so, reads to the next author as two subjects that were never related — and they will not know to look for the other half." Cheap, and it converts a silent failure into a visible one.

**Re-attach context when splitting.** Anthropic's contextual-retrieval result — 35% failure reduction from contextualising chunks alone, 49% with contextual BM25 ([2024-09-19](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval)) — is a measured mitigation for T4 in a retrieval pipeline. Interpretation for authored splits: each new document should be able to state its own subject without its former neighbours, which is the same requirement the atomicity standard's fifth test imposes from the other direction. Boundary: the measured benefit is for retrieval, not for reader comprehension, and I found no measurement of the latter.

**Record conflicts instead of resolving them.** This repository's corpus encodes T7's mitigation as a status value: `flagged` means "two same-claim-type authoritative sources contradict each other and a human has not yet resolved it", is "explicitly not a generic low-confidence marker", and carries the rule that "A node's own author MUST NOT resolve a `flagged` conflict by picking a side" (`launchpad/docs/corpus/standards/status.md` at `5f675645`). **Boundary condition, and it is a serious one, recorded in that same standard:** `validate.py` "contains no logic that reads or branches on a node's status value beyond generic JSON Schema enum membership checking", so this mitigation is an author-and-reviewer convention with no mechanical enforcement. Its own standard names issue #1410 as the open work to change that.

**Instruct explicitly for certainty preservation — it helps and does not solve.** Prompt-based intervention reduced news-rewriting distortion from 64.3% to 41.9%, and only explicit "preserve certainty" instructions approached directional neutrality ([Belem et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.07951v1)). A 22-point reduction that leaves two rewrites in five distorted is a mitigation, not a control.

**Do not assume an accuracy instruction helps — it can backfire.** Peters & Chin-Yee found summaries retrieved with accuracy instructions were *twice as likely* to contain generalized conclusions than under the simple prompt (OR = 1.90, 95% CI [1.11, 3.26], p = 0.02) ([2025, via PMC12042776](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12042776/), read 2026-09-08). This is the most counter-intuitive result in the report and it directly contradicts the most common mitigation people reach for. Their own supported recommendations are different in kind: lower temperature, prompts enforcing indirect past-tense reporting, and model choice — they report Claude as most accurate on this measure, which is a mutable product fact and a conflict of interest worth naming, since I am a Claude model reporting it.

**Re-derive from the original, not from the previous document.** Follows from T9 and from the local `README`/`AGENTS` case, where the second document's value was inherited from the first rather than re-measured. Trade-off: re-derivation is the expensive path, and for claims about a live system it is the only one that terminates.

**Reducing abstractiveness works, by not transforming.** Ladhak et al.'s result — faithfulness methods "fail to consistently improve over the control at the same level of abstractiveness" ([ACL 2022](https://aclanthology.org/2022.acl-long.100/)) — means a system tuned for fidelity will drift toward copying. Interpretation: this is a real mitigation and a real trap. A transformation pipeline evaluated only on faithfulness will converge on producing near-copies that pass every check while delivering none of the reason the transformation was requested. Any fidelity gate needs a compression or usefulness counterweight, or it optimises itself into a no-op.

## Limits and open questions

- **The genre gap is the report's principal limitation and it qualifies every quantitative figure above.** Every rate cited — 26–73%, 75%, 30%, 60% — was measured on news, scientific abstracts, radiology reports or QA benchmarks. Technical documentation differs in ways that could push the rates in either direction: it is more structured and more literal (which should help), and denser in preconditions, negations and version-bound claims (which should hurt). I found no study measuring transformation fidelity on a documentation corpus and treat the direction of every mechanism as well supported and every magnitude as untransferred.
- **Four load-bearing sources are not peer-reviewed.** Belem et al. (certainty distortion) and Santillana (coverage blindness) are 2026 preprints; the Anthropic result is a vendor engineering report with an incentive to motivate retrieval products; the local evidence is one repository. The certainty-distortion result in particular carries a lot of weight here on a single unreplicated chain, and its own metric correlates with humans at τ = 0.47.
- **T6 (fusion error) is supported in direction only.** A widely repeated "38% of fusion sentences contain incorrect facts" figure appears in secondary summaries; I could not verify it against Lebanoff et al.'s own text and have excluded it rather than cite it. The class rests on the two abstracts' qualitative claims.
- **T12 (homogenization) is the weakest class.** Its evidence is creative and argumentative writing with human co-authors, not agent transformation of documentation. It is plausible, it is the harm the question names that I would most expect to be real, and I have the least right to assert it. Whether a technical corpus can be shown to have lost genre distinction — as opposed to gained a house style — is open, and I did not find a metric that separates the two.
- **The local case is n = 1 and I chose it because it was clean.** It demonstrates that consistency-driven reconciliation can propagate error and that a guard can entrench it. It says nothing about how often that happens, here or anywhere. I did not survey this corpus for further instances, which would be a different and more useful study.
- **The chaining curve is unresolved.** Mohamed et al. report accumulation; Belem et al. report a plateau on scientific tasks and compounding on medical ones. Which shape documentation follows determines whether the reviewable moment is the first transformation or every one of them, and I cannot answer it.
- **Mutability.** The local observations are pinned to `5f675645` on 2026-09-09; the review-count contradiction is a live defect that someone may fix, at which point the reproduction above stops reproducing. The branch-protection figure it concerns is itself mutable. Model-specific results (which model generalizes least) are product facts with short half-lives.
- **Unresolved.** Does the omission-under-compression rate differ between documentation genres — does a runbook lose more than an API reference? Is there a detectable signature of a *destroyed disagreement*, i.e. can a merge that silently resolved a conflict be distinguished after the fact from a merge whose sources agreed? Can coverage checking be made tractable without a hand-authored oracle, for the restricted case where the source document is itself available? And does the first-transformation plateau, if real, mean an already-transformed corpus is beyond repair by any further transformation?

## Practical review checks

Research output, offered as candidates. None has been trialled here, and none is adopted policy.

- **Name the source revision and the operation in the output.** A transformed document should state what it was transformed from, at which revision, and by which operation. Without that, none of the checks below can be run at all, and the document's evidence relationship is unrecoverable.
- **Require a must-survive list before the transformation, not after.** Coverage cannot be checked without an oracle of what mattered, and after the fact the oracle is contaminated by the output. Asking the requester to list the claims, constraints and preconditions that must survive converts an unanswerable detection problem into an answerable one.
- **Diff the atomic-claim sets, not the prose.** Extract claims from source and output separately and compare the sets in both directions. Claims present only in the source are candidate omissions (T1); claims present only in the output are candidate fabrications; claims whose quantifiers or modality changed are candidate T2/T3.
- **Check modality and quantifier tokens mechanically.** `may`/`must`, `usually`/`always`, `in our tests`/unqualified, past/present tense, and the presence of a named population or configuration. This is cheap, deterministic, and aimed at the axis with the highest measured distortion rate.
- **Route absence, totality and generality changes to a human.** A transformation that turned a bounded observation into a general rule is the single best-evidenced harm in this report and is not reliably detectable by an entailment check, which the generalized form still passes.
- **On a split, require each output to state its own subject without its siblings.** The extractive-summarization result is that 30% of relocations break on coreference and discourse alone; a document that cannot say what it is about without its former neighbours has inherited that defect.
- **On a split, require a record of what was declined and where it went.** Converts the silent half of the split/merge asymmetry into a visible one.
- **On a merge, require the disagreements to be enumerated before the merged text is accepted.** If the sources agreed, the list is empty and the check is free. If they did not, the list is the most valuable output of the operation and is exactly what the merge would otherwise destroy.
- **Treat a merge that produced no conflict list from sources that were maintained separately as a finding.** Independent documents about the same subject usually disagree somewhere; silent perfect agreement is more likely to indicate the reconciliation was not looked for.
- **Never reconcile toward the more authoritative document; reconcile toward the better-evidenced claim.** The local case is a document that was right being corrected to match a document that was wrong because the latter was normative. Where the two come apart, re-derive from the underlying system rather than choosing between the documents.
- **Do not let a consistency check stand as a correctness check, and give it an expiry.** A guard that asserts two documents agree encodes whichever value was current when it was written. If such a check exists it should name the evidence for the value it enforces and be re-derived, not just re-run — and a consistency check that no pipeline runs is worse than none, because its existence implies coverage that is not there.
- **Verify that cross-references still point at the content they were drawn for, not merely that they resolve.** Structural validators cannot distinguish these, so this is a human check, and restructuring is the operation that requires it.
- **Do not add an accuracy instruction and count it as a mitigation.** The one controlled measurement available found it roughly doubled the odds of overgeneralization. Prefer measures with directional evidence: explicit certainty-preservation instructions, lower temperature, and past-tense reporting.
- **Pair any fidelity gate with a compression or usefulness measure.** A pipeline scored on faithfulness alone converges on near-copies that pass while delivering nothing.
